P[7][Heading 1]1. What is Artificial Intelligence (AI)?
P[8][Normal][BOLD] A computer normally follows exact instructions written by a person. Artificial Intelligence (AI) is different. It is a way of making computers and machines learn, think and make decisions in a smart way, a little like humans do.
P[9][Normal]Humans become smart by learning from experience. A child learns what a dog looks like by seeing many dogs. In the same way, AI becomes smart by learning from many examples. These examples are called data.
P[11][Heading 1]2. What is Data?
P[12][Normal][BOLD] Data means facts and information that can be collected, stored and used. Data is the food of AI. Without data, AI cannot learn anything.
P[13][Normal]Data can come in many forms:
P[15][Normal]Figure 2: Different kinds of data that AI can learn from.
P[17][Heading 1]3. What is a Pattern?
P[18][Normal][BOLD] A pattern is something that repeats or follows a rule. When we look at lots of data carefully, we can spot things that are similar or that happen again and again.
P[19][Heading 2]Patterns in daily life
P[20][List Paragraph]Every day the sun rises in the morning and sets in the evening.
P[21][List Paragraph]Number pattern:  2, 4, 6, 8, ...   The next number is 10 (we add 2 each time).
P[22][List Paragraph]Your school bell rings at the same times every day.
P[23][List Paragraph]Bananas are long, yellow and curved, while apples are round.
P[26][Normal]Figure 3: AI looks at many fruits and finds a pattern that separates apples from bananas.
P[27][Normal][BOLD] Key idea: AI does not memorise every example. It finds the pattern hidden in the data and then uses that pattern on new things it has never seen.
P[28][Heading 1]4. How Does AI Learn from Data?
P[29][Normal]AI learns step by step, like a student who practises and corrects mistakes. This is called training the AI.
P[31][Normal]Figure 4: The learning loop. AI repeats these steps again and again.
P[32][List Paragraph][BOLD] Give examples (data). We show the AI thousands of examples.
P[33][List Paragraph][BOLD] AI makes a guess. It tries to find a pattern and predict an answer.
P[34][List Paragraph][BOLD] Check the answer. The answer is compared with the correct one.
P[35][List Paragraph][BOLD] Learn from mistakes. If the guess is wrong, the AI adjusts itself and tries again.
P[37][Normal]After many rounds of practice, the AI makes fewer mistakes. It has become smart!

P[48][Heading 1]7. Why Good Data is Important
P[49][Normal][BOLD] AI is only as smart as the data it learns from. If the data is wrong or unfair, the AI will also make wrong or unfair decisions. Computer experts say: "Garbage in, garbage out."
P[51][Normal]Figure 6: Good data helps AI learn well. Bad data confuses it.
P[52][Normal][BOLD] Example: If a child is taught that a cat is called a 'dog', the child will always call cats dogs. AI is the same. Wrong labels in the data lead to wrong answers.

P[59][Heading 1]9. How a Computer Sees a Picture
P[60][Normal]When you look at a photo, you see a face, a tree or a heart. A computer does not see like we do. It sees only numbers.
P[61][Normal][BOLD] Every picture is made of tiny dots called pixels. Each pixel has a number that tells the computer its colour or brightness. In the simple heart below, 1 means a coloured square and 0 means a white square.
P[63][Normal]Figure 8: A picture is stored as numbers. The AI learns patterns in these numbers.
P[64][Heading 2]What does the AI do with these numbers?
P[65][List Paragraph]It looks at the numbers of thousands of pictures.
P[66][List Paragraph]It finds patterns. For example, the numbers for a heart always make a certain shape.
P[67][List Paragraph]When it gets a new picture, it compares the numbers with the patterns it has learned and decides what the picture shows.
P[69][Heading 1]10. Human Learning and AI Learning
P[70][Normal]Humans and AI both learn from examples, but there are important differences.
P[72][Normal][BOLD] Key idea: AI is a helper, not a replacement for people. Humans decide what to teach AI and check whether its answers are right.

P[76][Heading 1]12. Using AI Safely and Responsibly
P[77][Normal]AI is a powerful tool. Like any tool, we must use it wisely and kindly. Here are some golden rules for students.
P[78][List Paragraph][BOLD] Protect your privacy. Never share your full name, address, phone number, passwords or photos with an AI app or website without a trusted adult's permission.
P[79][List Paragraph][BOLD] Check the answer. AI can make mistakes. Always confirm important facts from a book, a teacher or a trusted website.
P[80][List Paragraph][BOLD] Do your own thinking. Use AI to help you learn, but do your homework and thinking yourself. Copying AI answers does not make you smarter.
P[81][List Paragraph][BOLD] Be fair and kind. Never use AI to make fun of, trick or hurt anyone.
P[82][List Paragraph][BOLD] Ask an adult. If something online or from an AI makes you feel uncomfortable, tell a parent or teacher.

Unit A: Introduction to Python
Coding a Solution
A1. What is Python?
Python is a popular high-level programming language. It is used to give computers clear instructions.
Python is known for its simple, readable style, which makes it a good first language for students.
Python was created by Guido van Rossum and first released in 1991. Its name was inspired by the
comedy group Monty Python, not by the snake.
A2. Why is Python popular?
 Its commands look similar to simple English.
 It can be used for websites, games, data analysis, automation and AI-related work.
 It has many free libraries, which are collections of ready-made code.
 It works on Windows, macOS and Linux.
 It is widely used in schools, universities and technology companies.
Python is often used with data and AI because it can process information, organise datasets, perform
calculations and connect to AI tools. However, an ordinary Python program is not automatically an AI
system. AI usually needs a trained model or an external AI service.

Everyday life
Automating repeated tasks, such as making a shopping list
AI and data
Preparing data, performing calculations and using AI libraries or services
A4. Programs, languages and source code
A program is a set of instructions that tells a computer what to do. A programming language, such
as Python, is the language we use to write those instructions. The written instructions are called
source code.
Computers follow instructions exactly. They do not guess meanings. If an instruction is unclear or
written incorrectly, the computer may show an error or produce an unexpected result.

B1. How people interact with AI
Artificial Intelligence (AI) refers to computer systems that can perform tasks often associated with
human intelligence, such as recognising speech, answering questions or recommending videos.
People communicate with AI by giving it information or instructions.
For example, a student may ask a voice assistant, “What is the capital of Japan?” The AI processes
the spoken question and responds with an answer.

people understand the program.

1. Introduction to AI
What is Artificial Intelligence?
1.1 What is AI?
Artificial Intelligence (AI) is a branch of computer science that creates computer systems which can
perform tasks that usually need human intelligence. These tasks include recognising faces,
understanding speech, translating languages, recommending videos and identifying objects in
photos.
AI does not “think” exactly like a person. It follows mathematical patterns found in large amounts of
data. For example, an AI system can learn that emails containing certain words are often spam, and
then use that pattern to filter new emails.
Key idea
AI learns patterns from examples (data) and uses those patterns to make useful predictions or decisions.

The fuel that powers AI
2.1 What is data?
Data is information collected about people, things, events or places. Data can be numbers, words,
pictures, sounds, dates or sensor readings. AI needs data because it learns by finding regularities,
called patterns, in examples.
Example: A weather app collects data such as temperature, humidity, wind speed and rainfall. From
past data, it can estimate tomorrow’s weather.

3. How AI Learns: Machine Learning
From examples to predictions
3.1 What is machine learning?
Machine learning (ML) is a method used by AI systems to learn rules and patterns from data instead
of being programmed with every rule by hand. The more good-quality examples the system sees, the
better it can become at recognising patterns.
For example, instead of writing rules such as “if a fruit is red and round, call it an apple,” we can show
the computer many labelled fruit photos. The machine learning system finds useful patterns by itself.

3.3 Training data and testing data
Training data is the set of examples an AI uses to learn. Testing data is a separate set of examples
used to check how well the AI performs on new data. We never let the AI see the testing answers
while it is learning.
Why separate the data?
If we test an AI using the same examples it learned from, it may look perfect simply because it has memorised
those examples. Testing with new examples shows whether it has truly learned a useful pattern.
3.4 AI models
An AI model is the trained program that stores the patterns learned from data. Think of it as a
“pattern-finder.” When we give it new features, it gives an output called a prediction.

3.5 Evaluating an AI model
Evaluation means checking how often the AI’s predictions are correct. A common measure is
accuracy.

4. How AI Makes Choices
Classification, recommendations and errors
4.1 Classification
Classification is when AI places something into a category. The AI looks at features and chooses
the most likely label.
Example
Categories
Features used

4.2 Recommendations
A recommendation system suggests items a person may like. It uses patterns from your past
choices and from people with similar choices. For example, if you often watch science videos, the
system may recommend more science videos.
Recommendations are not always “right” or “wrong” in the same way as classification. Instead, we
ask whether they are useful, relevant and fair.
4.3 Prediction errors
AI can make two main kinds of mistakes:
 False positive: The AI says “yes” when the correct answer is “no.” Example: an email filter marks
a normal message as spam.
 False negative: The AI says “no” when the correct answer is “yes.” Example: a spam filter
misses an actual spam email.
 Wrong category: In classification, the AI chooses the incorrect label. Example: it calls a lime an
apple.
4.4 Why errors happen
 The training data was too small.
 The examples were unclear or labelled incorrectly.
 The new example is very different from the training data.
 Important features were missing.
 The data was biased or unbalanced.
Important
A high accuracy score does not always mean an AI is fair or safe. We must also check who is included in the
data, what mistakes it makes, and who could be harmed by those mistakes.

6. Accuracy, Data Quality and Responsible AI
Making AI helpful, fair and safe
6.1 Accuracy and incorrect predictions
Accuracy tells us the percentage of correct predictions. But accuracy alone is not enough. We
should also look at which examples the AI gets wrong.
For example, a fruit AI may be 90% accurate overall but always misidentifies green apples. That
specific error could cause problems in a fruit-sorting factory.
6.2 Data quality
Good-quality data
Poor-quality data
Correct labels
Wrong or missing labels

6.3 Bias and fairness
Bias is an unfair pattern in an AI system, often caused by unbalanced or incomplete data. If a
face-recognition system is trained mostly on photos of people from one region, it may work poorly for
people from other regions.
Fairness means the AI should work reasonably well for different groups of people and should not
unfairly disadvantage anyone.
6.4 Privacy
Privacy means protecting personal information. AI often uses data about people, so we must collect
only necessary data, keep it secure, and ask permission where required.
6.5 Responsible AI
 Use accurate, diverse and permission-based data.
 Test AI with many different groups and situations.
 Keep humans involved in important decisions.
 Explain AI decisions clearly when possible.
 Protect personal and private information.
 Correct errors and improve the system regularly.
Golden rule
AI should assist humans—not replace human judgement, care and responsibility.

9. Classroom Activities
Learn by doing—no computer required
Activity 1: Human vs AI Classification
Objective
Classify everyday items using human reasoning and compare it with how an AI classifier works.
Materials: Paper cards, pen, table below.
Steps:
 Your teacher will give each group 12 cards with items such as “mango,” “car,” “rose,” “dog,” “bus,”
and “tulip.”
 Sort the cards into two groups: Living things and Non-living things.
 Write the feature you used for each card, such as “grows,” “moves by itself,” or “made by
humans.”
 Now imagine an AI trained only on pictures. Which features could it use?
 Discuss one item that may confuse the AI and explain why.
Student task: Complete the table.
Item
Group
Feature you used
Could AI get confused? Why?
Expected outcome: Students understand that both humans and AI use features, but humans also
use context, experience and common sense.
Discussion questions: What features were most useful? When can an AI make a mistake even with
clear features?
Teacher guidance: Encourage students to justify every grouping with a feature. Highlight that AI
does not “know” meanings; it detects statistical patterns.

Activity 2: Build a Mini Dataset
Objective
Create a small labelled dataset and identify features and patterns.
Materials: Notebook, ruler, coloured pencils.
Steps:
 Choose a topic: school bags, leaves, shoes, or tiffin boxes.
 Collect information about 8 examples. Choose at least three features.
 Add a label for each example.
 Write two patterns you notice.
 State one example that does not fit the pattern.
Student task: Complete your dataset.
Example
Feature 1
Feature 2
Feature 3
Label
Pattern 1: ______________________________________________________________
Pattern 2: ______________________________________________________________
Example that does not fit: _______________________________________________
Expected outcome: Students can distinguish features from labels and recognise simple patterns.
Teacher guidance: Ask groups to swap datasets and find one additional pattern. Remind them that a
pattern from a small dataset may not always be true.

Activity 3: Train a Classroom Classifier
Objective
Simulate how an AI learns rules from training examples and predicts labels for new examples.
Materials: Paper slips, two boxes labelled “Training” and “Testing.”
Steps:
 Create 10 paper slips describing animals using only three features: number of legs, body
covering, and sound.
 Write the correct animal name on the back of each slip as its label.
 Put 7 slips in the Training box and 3 in the Testing box.
 As a class, read the training slips and write rules such as “four legs + fur + barking = dog.”
 Use the rules to predict labels for the testing slips.
 Turn over the slips and check the answers.
Student task: Write two classification rules your class discovered.
Rule 1: ______________________________________________________________________
Rule 2: ______________________________________________________________________
Expected outcome: Students experience training, testing, prediction and evaluation without a
computer.
Discussion questions: Which rule was most reliable? What would happen if one training label was
wrong?
Teacher guidance: Use this to introduce overfitting informally: rules that fit only the training
examples may fail on new examples.

Activity 5: Draw an AI Decision-Making Flowchart
Objective
Show the steps an AI follows from input data to a decision.
Scenario: An AI helps a school library decide whether a book is suitable for Grade 8 students. It
examines reading level, topic and content warnings.
Steps:
 Draw a start box: “New book arrives.”
 Add feature boxes: reading level, topic, content warnings.
 Add a decision box: “Does the book match Grade 8 reading level?”
 Add two paths: Yes → Recommend; No → Suggest another book.
 Add a final box: “Human librarian reviews the decision.”
Student task: Draw your flowchart in the space below. Use arrows and label every box.
Expected outcome: A labelled flowchart showing data input, feature checking, decision, output and
human review.
Teacher guidance: Accept any clear sequence that includes features, a decision point, an output
and human oversight.
